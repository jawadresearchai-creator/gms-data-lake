# The Limits of First-Mover Advantage in Corporate Science: Entry Timing, Persistence, and International Knowledge Networks

## Introduction

Firms increasingly participate directly in the production of scientific knowledge. Corporate researchers publish papers, collaborate with universities and other organizations, and connect scientific advances to downstream technological search. This involvement can improve a firm's ability to recognize external knowledge, evaluate its relevance, and translate it into invention, but it also exposes the firm to spillovers and requires resources to be committed well before the technological or commercial value of a scientific trajectory is fully known (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990; Fabrizio, 2009; Arora et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2024). These features create a strategic problem that is distinct from the familiar decision of when to enter a product market: **when should a firm begin participating in an emerging scientific domain?**

A conventional first-mover argument gives one answer. Entering early can create cumulative learning, privileged access to scarce resources, reputational benefits, and positions that later entrants must overcome (Lieberman & Montgomery, 1988). In scientific settings, early participation may likewise allow firms to accumulate topic-specific expertise, develop relationships with frontier researchers, and learn how a new body of knowledge relates to their existing technological capabilities. Evidence from individual scientists suggests that pioneering researchers can enjoy persistent publication advantages, while research on the private value of public science shows that firms can capture greater value when they use scientific advances before competitors do (Sabatier & Chollet, 2017; Arora et al., 2023). From this perspective, earlier corporate entry into a scientific field should create an advantage that compounds over time.

Yet the transfer of first-mover logic from markets to corporate science is not straightforward. Scientific entry often takes place when uncertainty is especially high. The dominant methods of a field may still be changing, technically promising pathways may turn out to be dead ends, complementary assets may be immature, and the commercial relevance of the underlying knowledge may remain unclear. Firms that wait can learn from the experiments of pioneers and avoid some of the costs of premature commitment. Entry-timing research has long recognized that first movers can experience disadvantages as well as advantages, and later work has emphasized that observed first-mover performance reflects both timing and underlying differences in firm resources and information (Lieberman & Montgomery, 1998; Cirik & Makadok, 2023). Empirical studies of emerging technologies similarly report mixed or nonlinear relationships between entry timing and innovation outcomes rather than a universal premium to being first (Park et al., 2020).

The tension is especially important for corporate scientific activity because scientific knowledge is only partially appropriable. Corporate research can contribute to a firm's downstream inventions, but published findings also spill over to rivals (Arora et al., 2021). Thus, the strategic return to entering a nascent scientific domain early depends not only on how much a firm learns but also on whether it possesses the capabilities needed to interpret, integrate, and continue exploiting a rapidly changing body of external knowledge. This suggests that **early entry may create an opportunity rather than an advantage by itself**.

One capability that may shape this opportunity is a firm's external scientific network. Scientific knowledge is geographically and organizationally distributed, and collaboration connects firms to heterogeneous knowledge pools that cannot be reproduced solely through internal search. Internationally diverse collaboration networks may expose a firm to a wider set of emerging ideas, enable earlier detection of scientific opportunities, and provide alternative sources of expertise as a field evolves. This logic is closely related to absorptive capacity: firms benefit from external knowledge when they can recognize, assimilate, transform, and exploit it (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990; Zahra & George, 2002). Basic research and university collaboration can improve both the timing and quality of innovative search (Fabrizio, 2009), while broader collaboration research shows that network effects are often contingent on the firm's capacity to absorb and recombine externally sourced knowledge (Najafi-Tavani et al., 2018; Moon et al., 2022).

Existing research leaves two distinctions insufficiently resolved. First, the literature has primarily examined first-mover advantage in product or technology markets, pioneering behavior among individual scientists, or the early **use** of public science in invention. Much less is known about whether firms obtain persistent advantage simply by entering the **production of science** earlier across many emerging scientific domains. Second, prior work often treats networks as antecedents of innovation performance without distinguishing whether network diversity makes a firm enter earlier or instead changes what happens **after** early entry. These are different theoretical mechanisms. A network that helps a firm detect opportunities sooner is a selection mechanism; a network that helps a firm survive uncertainty after entering is a conditioning mechanism.

We address these issues by studying corporate entry into emerging scientific topics at the firm-topic level. The design identifies globally emerging scientific domains from longitudinal topic trajectories, measures each firm's entry relative to the first corporate entrant observed in that topic, and follows subsequent scientific output, persistence, and normalized citation impact. Pre-entry international collaboration-network diversity is measured separately from entry timing. The primary analysis uses overlap weighting on genuine pre-entry firm characteristics, absorbed fixed effects, multiple cluster-robust inference procedures, and a prespecified grid of alternative emergence definitions and early-entry windows. This structure is important because the definition of an "emerging" scientific field and of an "early" entrant is inherently contestable; treating alternative defensible definitions as part of the analysis reduces the risk that a favorable specification determines the scientific conclusion.

The study makes four contributions. First, it extends entry-timing theory upstream from product and technology markets into corporate participation in scientific knowledge domains and tests whether the assumption of durable first-mover advantage applies in that setting. Second, it distinguishes **entry timing** from **persistence**, treating sustained participation as an organizational outcome that requires continued commitment after the initial entry event. Third, it separates the role of international scientific networks as a potential antecedent of earlier entry from their role as a potential moderator of post-entry persistence. Fourth, it incorporates specification and inference stability directly into the interpretation of first-mover effects rather than treating robustness as a secondary appendix exercise. Together, these contributions develop a more contingent view of scientific first-mover advantage in which timing, firm capabilities, and external knowledge access must be considered jointly.

## Theory and Hypotheses

### Scientific entry timing and subsequent scientific output

First-mover theory identifies several mechanisms through which early entrants can establish persistent advantages. Learning effects arise because earlier entrants have more time to accumulate experience and improve routines. Preemption effects occur when pioneers secure scarce resources, relationships, locations, or other positions that followers cannot obtain on equivalent terms. Early entry can also influence reputation and shape the structure of a developing market or technological field (Lieberman & Montgomery, 1988). Later work linked these mechanisms more explicitly to firm heterogeneity, emphasizing that first-mover outcomes depend on the resources and capabilities an entrant brings to the opportunity (Lieberman & Montgomery, 1998).

These mechanisms have plausible analogues in corporate science. Scientific research is cumulative: solving new problems depends on familiarity with prior work, methods, data, instrumentation, and tacit knowledge. A firm that enters a scientific domain early can begin building topic-specific human capital before later entrants. Its researchers can develop routines for evaluating competing approaches, accumulate relationships with relevant scientific communities, and learn which problems connect most strongly to the firm's technological base. Over time, those accumulated capabilities may lower the marginal cost of producing additional research within the domain.

The importance of prior scientific engagement for search is well established. Cohen and Levinthal (1990) argued that prior related knowledge increases the ability to recognize and exploit new external information. Fabrizio (2009) showed that firms' own research and collaboration with university scientists can improve innovative search, including its timing. Arora et al. (2023) similarly found that corporate participation in science predicts firms' ability to use public scientific knowledge before rivals. Although these studies do not examine firm-level entry into emerging scientific topics in the same way as the present design, they support the idea that participating earlier can generate familiarity and learning that later entrants lack.

Evidence at the researcher level also points toward cumulative pioneering advantages. Sabatier and Chollet (2017), studying nanotechnology researchers, found that pioneers produced more subsequent scientific output even after addressing endogenous pioneering behavior. If analogous accumulation operates within firms, early corporate entry should allow a firm to establish a larger and more durable research presence in a topic.

There are countervailing mechanisms. Early entrants face greater uncertainty, may invest in trajectories that later prove unproductive, and may incur costs while methods and standards remain unstable. Later entrants can observe which approaches survive and can imitate successful research directions. These possibilities make a first-mover advantage theoretically contestable rather than automatic. Nevertheless, the cumulative-learning and capability-building logic yields the ex ante prediction that earlier entrants will generate greater subsequent scientific activity.

**H1: Earlier relative corporate entry into an emerging scientific domain is associated with higher subsequent firm-topic scientific output.**

### Scientific entry timing and persistence

Scientific output and scientific persistence are related but conceptually distinct. Output measures the volume of subsequent activity; persistence asks whether a firm remains active in the domain after the initial entry episode. A firm may publish several papers shortly after entering and then withdraw, while another may sustain a smaller but enduring research program. Persistence therefore captures an important organizational dimension of scientific commitment.

First-mover theory implies that early entry can increase persistence through path dependence. Once a firm has hired or developed specialized researchers, built external relationships, established internal routines, and linked a new scientific domain to organizational problem-solving, exiting that domain may become less attractive. Early entrants also have more time to establish legitimacy and relational positions within the emerging community. These investments can generate switching costs and increase the future productivity of continued participation.

Absorptive-capacity theory reinforces this argument. Knowledge accumulated through research increases the ability to value and use subsequent knowledge (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990). This recursive feature creates a potential cumulative advantage: research undertaken today raises the return to research and external knowledge acquisition tomorrow. In an emerging field, firms that begin this process sooner should reach higher levels of topic-specific capability before followers enter.

However, persistence also makes the liabilities of early entry more visible. Firms often learn about the true quality of an opportunity only after entering. A pioneer may discover that the field is technically immature, weakly connected to its downstream businesses, or costly to integrate with existing capabilities. Later entrants can condition their decisions on information produced by earlier experimentation. Theoretical models of entry timing therefore highlight a trade-off between preemption and learning from others: competitive pressure encourages earlier action, while informational benefits can reward waiting. Emerging scientific domains should contain both forces.

Our directional hypothesis follows the first-mover/cumulative-learning logic: if early entry creates topic-specific capability that compounds, early entrants should be more likely to remain active in the field.

**H2: Earlier relative corporate entry into an emerging scientific domain is associated with greater persistence of firm activity in that domain.**

### Scientific entry timing and scientific impact

A scientific first-mover advantage may appear not only in how much research a firm produces or how long it remains active, but also in the influence of its research. Emerging domains contain unsettled questions, underdeveloped methods, and fewer established competitors. Early entrants may therefore have greater opportunities to define problems, establish approaches, and produce work that becomes foundational as the field expands.

Several mechanisms support this prediction. First, pioneering firms can select from a broader set of unsolved scientific problems before later researchers occupy those niches. Second, early entry can increase visibility within a growing community, making a firm's work more likely to become part of the intellectual reference structure used by later entrants. Third, cumulative learning may allow early entrants to improve the quality of subsequent research while retaining superior familiarity with the field's origins and key uncertainties.

The prediction is consistent with the broader literature linking corporate science to downstream knowledge creation. Arora et al. (2021) show that corporate scientific research can create private value through its use in firms' inventions, even as knowledge spills over to competitors. Chen et al. (2024) find that stronger linkages between corporate scientific publications and patents are associated with greater technological innovation and that firms benefit from scientific knowledge produced at earlier stages. These studies demonstrate that the timing and integration of scientific knowledge can matter for value creation, although they do not establish that early topic entry necessarily produces more influential scientific publications.

Citation outcomes also raise important empirical issues. Raw citations mechanically favor older publications and vary dramatically across scientific fields. Consequently, a valid test must separate a true impact difference from citation-age and field-composition effects. Our empirical design therefore uses field-year-normalized citation impact and imposes within-topic support rules rather than comparing raw citation counts across unrelated topics.

The ex ante pioneering logic nevertheless implies that earlier entrants should, on average, produce more influential post-entry science.

**H3: Earlier relative corporate entry into an emerging scientific domain is associated with higher field-year-normalized post-entry citation impact.**

### International scientific network diversity and earlier entry

The preceding hypotheses treat entry timing as a strategic position. A separate question is why some firms enter earlier than others. Firm resources and organizational characteristics have long been associated with entry timing in new technological settings, implying that pioneering behavior is at least partly endogenous. Sabatier and Chollet (2017) similarly found that pioneering scientists were more likely to possess established careers, strong collaboration networks, and access to field-specific resources before entering nanotechnology. These findings suggest that network structure may be an antecedent to early entry rather than merely a consequence of it.

International scientific collaboration can expand a firm's opportunity-sensing capacity. Scientific discoveries are geographically distributed, and emerging topics often develop simultaneously across institutions and countries. A firm whose researchers collaborate across a more diverse set of national scientific communities may encounter heterogeneous ideas earlier and may be less dependent on the information available in a single local network. Such exposure can reduce informational blind spots and increase the probability of recognizing a promising scientific trajectory before it becomes widely established.

This argument follows the acquisition side of absorptive capacity. Cohen and Levinthal (1990) emphasized that external knowledge matters only when organizations can recognize its value, while Zahra and George (2002) distinguished the acquisition and assimilation of knowledge from its later transformation and exploitation. International network diversity can increase the range of knowledge available for acquisition. Firms with broader scientific connections may therefore receive more early signals about new domains and be better positioned to evaluate whether those signals deserve research investment.

At the same time, diversity is not costless. Heterogeneous networks can increase coordination complexity, create communication barriers, and expose firms to more knowledge than they can effectively process. Network effects on innovation are consequently often contingent rather than uniformly positive. Najafi-Tavani et al. (2018), for example, show that collaboration benefits depend on absorptive capacity, while Moon et al. (2022) demonstrate that the innovation effects of network configurations depend on tie structure and firms' ability to absorb knowledge.

Despite these costs, the opportunity-sensing logic predicts that broader international scientific exposure should increase the likelihood that a firm identifies and enters an emerging topic near the corporate frontier.

**H4: Greater pre-entry international collaboration-network diversity is associated with earlier relative corporate entry into emerging scientific domains.**

### International scientific network diversity as a condition for persistent early entry

Network diversity may be even more consequential **after** a firm enters an emerging scientific field. Early entrants confront high uncertainty precisely because the field is still forming. Relevant expertise may be dispersed, dominant approaches may not yet be known, and the focal firm may need to revise its research trajectory repeatedly as new evidence emerges. Under these conditions, access to heterogeneous external knowledge can act as a portfolio of scientific options.

A diverse international network can help an early entrant in several ways. It can provide alternative technical perspectives when one trajectory fails, expose the firm to complementary methods developed elsewhere, and connect internal researchers to communities that move at different speeds or emphasize different applications. Such breadth may also reduce dependence on a small number of partners and allow the firm to reconfigure its scientific relationships as the domain evolves.

This logic is not simply that "more ties are better." External knowledge must be interpreted and integrated. Absorptive-capacity research argues that prior knowledge and internal research routines determine whether firms can convert external information into useful action (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990; Zahra & George, 2002). Fabrizio (2009) similarly shows complementarity between firms' internal research and external university relationships. Network research extends this point: collaboration can improve innovation performance, but the effect depends on organizational capabilities and the configuration of external ties (Najafi-Tavani et al., 2018; Moon et al., 2022).

This produces an important distinction between H4 and H5. If network diversity primarily improves opportunity detection, it should predict earlier entry. If instead its main value is to supply heterogeneous knowledge after a firm has committed to a nascent field, network diversity may have little effect on who enters first but may determine which early entrants remain active. The second mechanism is a **conditioning** effect rather than a selection effect.

The conditioning logic is also consistent with first-mover research that treats entry timing effects as capability-dependent. Lieberman and Montgomery (1998) explicitly linked first-mover advantage to firm resources, and more recent work continues to emphasize that early entry can produce different outcomes depending on organizational and technological conditions. In emerging technologies, Park et al. (2020) found that early-mover effects differed across funding and innovation outcomes and were moderated by knowledge-spillover activity. These patterns suggest that timing is unlikely to generate a uniform return independent of the firm's knowledge architecture.

For corporate science, the relevant architecture is the set of scientific relationships through which the firm can access and recombine knowledge. Early entrants with internationally diverse networks should therefore be better equipped to adapt as an emerging domain changes and, as a result, should exhibit greater persistence than equally early entrants whose external scientific knowledge access is narrower.

**H5: Pre-entry international collaboration-network diversity positively moderates the association between earlier entry and subsequent persistence.**

## Conceptual Summary

The theoretical model separates three elements that are often conflated in studies of first-mover advantage. **Entry timing** captures when the firm becomes an observable scientific participant relative to other corporate entrants. **Scientific advantage** is multidimensional and includes subsequent output, persistence, and normalized impact. **Network capability** captures pre-entry access to heterogeneous external scientific knowledge. H1–H3 test whether earlier entry is associated with later scientific advantage, H4 tests whether network diversity predicts earlier entry, and H5 tests whether network diversity conditions the relationship between timing and persistence.

This separation allows the analysis to distinguish an automatic first-mover model from a capability-contingent model. Under an automatic model, earlier entry should improve outcomes regardless of external knowledge architecture. Under a capability-contingent model, the value of early entry depends on what the firm can learn, integrate, and reconfigure after entering. The empirical analysis evaluates the five ex ante hypotheses without redefining them in response to the observed results.
