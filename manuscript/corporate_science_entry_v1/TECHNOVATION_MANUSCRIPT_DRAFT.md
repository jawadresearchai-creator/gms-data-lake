## Abstract

Firms increasingly participate directly in emerging scientific domains, yet it remains unclear whether earlier participation produces a durable corporate scientific advantage. We examine relative entry into observable corporate scientific production and distinguish the value of entry timing from the role of pre-entry international scientific collaboration networks. The frozen primary panel contains 171 firm-topic entry episodes across 54 scientific topics and 21 linked firms; 65 episodes entered within two years of the first linked corporate entrant and 106 entered later. We combine overlap weighting on pre-entry firm characteristics with absorbed fixed effects, multiple small-cluster inference procedures, and a prespecified sensitivity grid of alternative emergence and early-entry definitions. Earlier relative entry is not associated with higher subsequent topic output or greater three-year persistence. The primary citation-impact contrast lacks sufficient within-topic support, and support-qualified sensitivity estimates are near zero. Pre-entry international network diversity also does not predict earlier relative entry. However, the interaction between earlier entry and pre-entry network diversity is positive across most frozen definitions and all leave-one-firm-out refits, although its statistical support is sensitive to the inference method. These results do not support an automatic first-mover advantage in observable corporate science. Instead, they suggest a capability-contingent view in which early participation may create an opportunity whose sustainability depends partly on the external knowledge architecture the firm brings to the domain. The evidence is associational rather than causal.

**Keywords:** corporate science; entry timing; technological innovation; international collaboration; scientific networks; absorptive capacity; persistence; emerging scientific domains

## 1. Introduction

Firms do not only consume scientific knowledge; many participate directly in producing it. Corporate researchers publish scientific papers, collaborate with universities and other research organizations, and connect scientific advances to downstream technological search. Such participation can help firms recognize and evaluate external knowledge and link scientific discovery to invention, but it also exposes knowledge to spillovers and requires resources to be committed before the technological or commercial value of a scientific trajectory is fully known (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990; Fabrizio, 2009; Arora et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2024). For science-intensive firms, this creates a strategic timing problem: **when should a firm begin observable participation in an emerging scientific domain?**

A conventional first-mover argument favors earlier participation. Early entrants can accumulate experience, secure relationships and reputational positions, and build routines before followers arrive (Lieberman & Montgomery, 1988). Scientific research is cumulative, so firms that enter earlier may build topic-specific knowledge, develop ties with frontier researchers, and learn how a new body of science connects to existing technological capabilities. Evidence from individual scientists suggests that pioneers can retain publication advantages, while research on firms' use of public science shows that firms can capture greater value when they use scientific advances before rivals do (Sabatier & Chollet, 2017; Arora et al., 2023).

Yet corporate science differs from a product market in ways that make early-mover advantage uncertain. Emerging scientific domains are precisely those in which methods, standards, applications, and technical trajectories remain unsettled. Early entrants can commit to pathways that later prove unproductive, whereas later entrants can observe the experiments of pioneers and avoid some costs of premature commitment. Entry-timing research therefore recognizes both first-mover advantages and disadvantages, and contemporary theory distinguishes observed first-mover advantage from the counterfactual first-mover benefit that would arise if the same firm could be observed entering at different times (Lieberman & Montgomery, 1998; Cirik & Makadok, 2023). Empirical work on emerging technologies similarly finds mixed or nonlinear timing-performance relationships rather than a universal premium to being first (Park et al., 2020).

Corporate scientific entry also occurs in a setting of incomplete appropriability. Published corporate science can contribute to downstream invention while simultaneously spilling knowledge to competitors (Arora et al., 2021). Thus, the strategic value of entering early may depend not only on the time at which a firm appears in a domain but also on the capabilities it can mobilize after entry. In this sense, early participation may create an **opportunity** rather than an **advantage by itself**.

A firm's external scientific network is a plausible capability-related condition. Scientific knowledge is geographically and organizationally distributed. International collaboration can connect a firm to heterogeneous knowledge pools, multiple technical communities, and alternative sources of expertise. Such diversity could matter before entry by helping a firm detect an emerging opportunity earlier, or after entry by helping the firm adapt as an uncertain domain evolves. These are theoretically distinct mechanisms. The first is a **selection mechanism**—network diversity helps explain who enters earlier. The second is a **conditioning mechanism**—network diversity changes what happens to firms after they enter early. Absorptive-capacity research provides a foundation for both possibilities because external knowledge contributes to innovation only when firms can recognize, assimilate, and exploit it (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990; Zahra & George, 2002; Fabrizio, 2009).

Prior research leaves this distinction unresolved. Entry-timing studies focus primarily on product or technology markets; science studies have examined pioneering individual researchers; and corporate-science research has investigated the value of scientific research or firms' early use of public science. Much less is known about whether firms obtain persistent advantage simply by entering the **observable production of science** earlier across many emerging domains. Moreover, collaboration networks are often treated as general antecedents of innovation without separating whether they affect entry timing or condition persistence after entry.

We address these gaps using a firm-topic design that links SEC issuer information to OpenAlex-derived corporate science. Global topic trajectories are used to identify scientific emergence independently of firms. Each linked firm's first observed publication activity in a topic is then located relative to the first observed corporate entry among linked firms in that same topic. We follow subsequent topic output, persistence, and normalized citation impact and measure international collaboration-network diversity during the three years preceding entry. Importantly, the corporate adoption frontier is **sample-relative**: it is the first entrant observed among the linked firms, not a claim to identify the first corporation worldwide.

The study makes three contributions. First, it establishes a boundary test for first-mover logic in corporate science by asking whether earlier observable scientific participation is associated with durable scientific advantage across emerging domains. Second, it distinguishes arrival from continuation by separating entry timing, subsequent output, persistence, and impact, and by separating the network selection mechanism from the network conditioning mechanism. Third, it incorporates operational and inferential uncertainty into the result itself. Emerging topics and early entry can be defined in multiple defensible ways; rather than selecting a favorable definition after seeing outcomes, we froze the primary design and evaluated a prespecified grid of feasible alternatives.

The results challenge an automatic first-mover account. Earlier relative entry does not show a general output or persistence premium, and pre-entry network diversity does not predict earlier entry. The one recurring positive pattern is conditional: the early-entry–persistence association becomes more favorable as pre-entry international network diversity increases. That interaction is directionally stable across most frozen designs and all leave-one-firm-out refits, but its statistical support is sensitive to small-cluster inference. We therefore interpret it as qualified theory-building evidence rather than confirmation of a network-enabled first-mover effect.

## 2. Theory and hypotheses

### 2.1 Entry timing and corporate scientific advantage

First-mover theory identifies several mechanisms through which earlier entry can generate durable advantage. Earlier entrants can learn before competitors, preempt scarce positions and relationships, shape developing expectations, and accumulate experience that followers must replicate (Lieberman & Montgomery, 1988). Later work emphasized that these advantages depend on the resources and capabilities firms bring to the opportunity and that early entry can also create disadvantages when uncertainty is high (Lieberman & Montgomery, 1998).

These mechanisms have direct analogues in scientific production. Scientific work depends on prior knowledge, methods, tacit routines, instruments, data, and relationships. A firm entering a domain near the corporate adoption frontier can begin building topic-specific human capital and search routines before later corporate participants. It can learn which scientific questions are productive, how the domain relates to internal technological problems, and which external researchers and institutions hold relevant expertise. Because absorptive capacity is cumulative, scientific work performed today can increase the ability to understand and exploit external knowledge tomorrow (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990).

Corporate research also improves firms' access to science. Fabrizio (2009) shows that internal research and collaboration with university scientists can improve innovative search, while Arora et al. (2023) show that participation in science predicts firms' ability to use public scientific knowledge before rivals. At the individual level, Sabatier and Chollet (2017) report persistent production advantages among pioneering nanotechnology researchers. If similar cumulative processes operate at the firm-topic level, earlier corporate participants should subsequently produce more research, remain active longer, and potentially generate more influential work.

The counterargument is substantial. Scientific trajectories are highly uncertain when they emerge. Early firms may invest while dominant methods are unsettled, complementary technologies are immature, and commercial relevance remains ambiguous. Followers can learn from pioneers and avoid failed trajectories. The hypotheses below therefore represent the ex ante cumulative-learning predictions, not assumptions that first-mover advantage must exist.

**H1: Earlier relative corporate entry into an emerging scientific domain is associated with higher subsequent firm-topic scientific output.**

Persistence captures a distinct dimension of scientific commitment. A firm may publish several papers around entry but later leave the field. Conversely, a smaller program may persist because specialized researchers, routines, relationships, and downstream connections make continued participation valuable. If early entry creates path-dependent topic-specific capability, earlier entrants should be more likely to remain active after the initial episode.

**H2: Earlier relative corporate entry into an emerging scientific domain is associated with greater persistence of firm activity in that domain.**

Early entry may also affect scientific influence. Nascent domains contain unsettled questions and fewer established positions, potentially allowing pioneers to define problems or create work that becomes foundational as later participants arrive. Because raw citations vary with publication age and field, our empirical test uses field-year-normalized impact rather than raw citation counts.

**H3: Earlier relative corporate entry into an emerging scientific domain is associated with higher field-year-normalized post-entry citation impact.**

### 2.2 International scientific networks and earlier entry

Entry timing is partly endogenous because firms differ in resources, attention, and access to information. International scientific collaboration can broaden the set of signals available to a firm. Researchers connected across countries may encounter emerging methods and questions earlier, reduce dependence on one local scientific community, and expose the firm to heterogeneous expertise. From an opportunity-sensing perspective, a more diverse pre-entry network should increase the likelihood that a firm recognizes and enters a developing topic near the observed corporate frontier.

Absorptive-capacity theory supports this acquisition logic. Prior knowledge helps firms recognize valuable external information (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990), while Zahra and George (2002) distinguish acquisition and assimilation from later transformation and exploitation. International network diversity increases the breadth of potential knowledge inputs, although heterogeneous networks also create coordination and integration costs. Network effects therefore need not be uniformly positive (Najafi-Tavani et al., 2018; Moon et al., 2022).

**H4: Greater pre-entry international collaboration-network diversity is associated with earlier relative corporate entry into emerging scientific domains.**

### 2.3 International scientific networks as a persistence contingency

Network diversity may be more valuable after entry than before it. Early entrants face uncertainty because scientific domains are still forming. Relevant expertise may be dispersed across countries and institutions, technical directions may change rapidly, and a firm may need to revise its research path as new evidence appears. Diverse external connections can provide alternative technical perspectives, complementary methods, and multiple channels through which a firm can continue learning.

This logic does not imply that network breadth alone creates advantage. Firms must integrate external knowledge with internal expertise. Prior work shows that collaboration benefits depend on absorptive and innovation capabilities (Najafi-Tavani et al., 2018), and that network position affects innovation performance contingent on the firm's capacity to exploit ties (Moon et al., 2022). Thus, the strategic role of network diversity can differ across stages: it may fail to determine who arrives early yet still make early participation more sustainable once the firm has committed.

This distinction is important for entry-timing theory. If timing benefits are capability-dependent, the same early position can generate different outcomes depending on the knowledge architecture surrounding the firm. Park et al. (2020), for example, show that early-mover outcomes differ across performance dimensions and can be moderated by knowledge-spillover activity. In corporate science, international collaboration diversity provides a measurable form of pre-entry external knowledge access.

**H5: Pre-entry international collaboration-network diversity positively moderates the association between earlier entry and subsequent persistence.**

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 Data and unit of analysis

We use the frozen GMS `sec_openalex_v10` cross-domain production release, which links SEC issuer information with OpenAlex-derived corporate scientific activity. The release was frozen on 8 September 2026 and classified by the pipeline as verified/research-ready with zero recorded QA errors. The analysis uses global OpenAlex topic-year trajectories together with firm-topic output, firm-country collaboration, company-wide research and citation histories, and SEC issuer metadata.

The unit of analysis is a **firm × scientific-topic entry episode**. The primary panel contains 171 episodes across 54 scientific topics and 21 linked firms. Sixty-five episodes are classified as early and 106 as later. The observational structure provides within-topic and within-firm variation, but the number of firms remains modest; consequently, we use multiple small-cluster inference procedures and limit external-validity claims to the linked-firm sample.

### 3.2 Global scientific emergence and relative corporate entry

Global topic emergence is constructed independently of firms from the OpenAlex `WORK_YEAR_TOPIC` mart. The frozen primary emergence rule (`p2_100`) defines a topic's operational emergence year as the first year in which global output reaches at least 100 works and remains at or above 100 works in the immediately following calendar year. Emergence must occur between 1990 and 2021.

For each firm-topic pair, entry year is the first year in which the linked company records positive publication output in the topic. For each topic, the **observed corporate adoption frontier** is the earliest such entry among linked firms. Relative entry lag equals firm entry year minus this sample-relative frontier year. The primary early-entry definition (`w2`) classifies firms entering within zero to two years of the observed frontier as early and all later entrants as later.

The primary design is therefore `p2_100_w2`. Topics must contain at least one early and one later linked entrant in the outcome-complete sample. The frontier measure should not be interpreted as identifying the first corporation worldwide; it is a relative timing construct within the linked sample.

Primary entry episodes occur no later than 2022 so that three-year outcomes are completely observed through 2025. Partial 2026 outcomes are excluded. Five-year sensitivity outcomes are limited to episodes entering by 2020.

### 3.3 Pre-entry scientific capacity and international network diversity

We measure firm characteristics during the three complete years preceding topic entry (t−3 to t−1). Company-wide scientific capacity includes cumulative publication output, cumulative citations, and an output trajectory defined as the difference between log output at t−1 and t−3 divided by two.

International collaboration measures are built from collaborator-country publication counts over the same three-year window. The focal diversity measure is Shannon entropy of country work shares:

\[
Entropy_i=-\sum_c p_{ic}\ln(p_{ic}),
\]

where \(p_{ic}\) is the share of the firm's collaborative works associated with country \(c\). We also compute the number of collaborator countries, collaboration volume, and a one-minus-HHI diversity measure. Entropy is standardized for H4 and H5.

### 3.4 Outcomes

For H1, subsequent topic output is the log of one plus cumulative firm-topic publications in years t+1 through t+3. H2 measures persistence as an indicator that the firm records positive publication output in the same topic at t+3. H3 uses a work-weighted, field-year-normalized citation ratio over t+1 through t+3 and then applies log(1+x). The H3 model is estimated only when adequate within-topic early/later support exists. The frozen rule requires at least 30 supported observations across at least eight topics.

H4 reverses the model orientation and treats early-entry status as the dependent construct, with pre-entry network entropy as the focal predictor. H5 interacts early-entry status with standardized pre-entry network entropy in the t+3 persistence model.

### 3.5 Observed-selection adjustment

Because early and later entrants differ on observed pre-entry characteristics, we use overlap weighting based on a regularized logistic propensity model. The model includes pre-entry research output, citations, output trajectory, collaborator-country count, network diversity measures, collaboration volume, an indicator for observed network data, and two-digit SIC indicators. Propensity scores are truncated to [0.03, 0.97]. Early entrants receive weight \(1-\hat p\); later entrants receive weight \(\hat p\), with weights normalized to mean one.

Field age and global topic scale at the firm's entry year are intentionally excluded from the primary propensity model because they are mechanically related to relative timing and would partially adjust away the treatment construct. Post-weight observed balance is strong: the overlap-weight effective sample size is 134.0, and the maximum absolute weighted standardized mean difference is 0.044.

### 3.6 Fixed effects and inference

Primary H1, H2, and H5 models use overlap-weighted absorbed fixed effects for topic, firm, and entry year. H4 uses topic and firm fixed effects but excludes entry-year fixed effects because calendar timing is intrinsic to the dependent construct. H2 and H5 persistence models are estimated as fixed-effect linear-probability specifications.

Primary inference clusters by scientific topic using CRV1. Given 21 firms and 54 topics, we additionally report topic CRV3, two-way topic-and-firm CRV1, and a firm-cluster wild bootstrap with 9,999 replications and Webb weights. H5 is further examined under alternative prespecified fixed-effect structures and by leaving each firm out in turn.

The purpose of these procedures is to characterize uncertainty rather than to select whichever inference method produces statistical significance.

### 3.7 Prespecified operational sensitivity

Five global emergence definitions were crossed with early-entry windows of zero, one, two, and three years, generating 20 candidate designs before inferential sensitivity analysis. Candidate designs were required to pass a frozen feasibility gate covering minimum topic, episode, firm, early/later-group, and network-coverage requirements. Thirteen designs passed and entered the sensitivity analysis. The primary `p2_100_w2` definition remains primary regardless of sensitivity outcomes.

## 4. Results

### 4.1 Earlier entry does not show a general scientific-performance premium

H1 predicts greater subsequent topic output among earlier entrants. The adjusted primary coefficient is −0.045. It is close to zero and non-significant under topic CRV1 (p=.861), topic CRV3 (p=.926), two-way topic-and-firm clustering (p=.840), and the firm wild bootstrap (p=.306). Across the 13 gate-passing sensitivity definitions, approximately 77% of +3-year H1 coefficients are negative, with a median of about −0.062. H1 is not supported.

H2 predicts greater persistence. The primary coefficient on early entry is −0.147. It is non-significant under topic CRV1 (p=.217), topic CRV3 (p=.559), and two-way clustering (p=.191); the firm wild-bootstrap p-value is .064, but the coefficient remains negative. Eleven of 13 +3 sensitivity estimates are negative, and all 13 +5 persistence estimates are negative. These results do not support the hypothesized persistence premium. We do not interpret the directional pattern as evidence that early entry causally reduces persistence.

The primary H3 citation-impact contrast does not meet its frozen support requirement. Only 19 observations across seven topics retain both a defined normalized-impact measure and within-topic early/later support, below the minimum of 30 observations across eight topics. H3 is therefore not estimable in the primary design rather than accepted or rejected. Four support-qualified sensitivity designs produce estimates of −0.151, −0.010, 0.003, and 0.022; none is statistically distinguishable from zero across the hardened inference procedures. These sensitivity-only results provide no indication of a hidden citation-impact premium.

### 4.2 Network diversity does not explain earlier entry

H4 predicts that pre-entry international network diversity is associated with earlier relative entry. The adjusted standardized entropy coefficient is −0.059. It is not statistically distinguishable from zero under topic CRV1 (p=.588), topic CRV3 (p=.700), two-way clustering (p=.607), or the firm wild bootstrap (p=.683). The coefficient is negative in all 13 gate-passing sensitivity designs. H4 is not supported.

This result is theoretically important because it separates the network selection mechanism from the H5 conditioning mechanism. Firms with more internationally diverse observed scientific networks are not systematically the firms that enter closer to the linked corporate frontier after adjustment.

### 4.3 Network diversity provides qualified directional evidence as a persistence contingency

H5 predicts that network diversity strengthens the early-entry–persistence association. The primary interaction coefficient is +0.184. Topic CRV1 yields p=.022 and two-way topic-and-firm clustering yields p=.032. However, inference is materially weaker under topic CRV3 (p=.193) and the firm wild bootstrap (p=.217).

The sign is more stable than conventional statistical significance. The H5 interaction remains positive in all 21 leave-one-firm-out refits, with estimates ranging from approximately 0.034 to 0.235. Across the 13 gate-passing frozen definitions, 12 of 13 +3-year H5 interactions are positive; the sign is also positive in approximately 77% of +5-year definitions. At the same time, none of the +3 sensitivity definitions reaches p<.10 under topic CRV3.

We therefore classify H5 as **qualified directional support that is inference-sensitive**. The recurring sign makes a single-firm artifact unlikely, but the relatively small number of higher-level clusters prevents a strong claim about statistical precision.

### 4.4 Stability across frozen definitions

The operational sensitivity grid produces a coherent pattern even though it does not transform the study into a uniformly significant one. Earlier entry rarely shows a positive main-effect premium; the H4 network-selection prediction is directionally unsupported; and H5 shows a recurring positive interaction that is not robust to conservative small-cluster inference.

The relevant conclusion is therefore not that alternative specifications reveal a hidden significant effect. Rather, the grid shows that **coefficient direction and inferential strength carry different information**. H5's positive direction is broad; its statistical precision is not.

## 5. Discussion

### 5.1 Early corporate scientific entry is not automatically an advantage

The central finding is a boundary on conventional first-mover logic. Earlier relative entry into observable corporate scientific production is not associated with a general subsequent output or persistence premium in the linked-firm sample. H1 is effectively null, and H2 is predominantly negative across frozen definitions, including every +5 persistence specification. The evidence therefore challenges the assumption that simply arriving near an emerging scientific frontier is sufficient to create durable organizational advantage.

This does not imply that early scientific activity has no value. Entry can generate learning, relationships, and options. Rather, the results suggest that early positioning and the capability to sustain scientific participation are different organizational problems. Scientific trajectories are uncertain, and firms may discover only after entry that a domain is technically immature, weakly connected to downstream businesses, or difficult to integrate. Followers can observe pioneers and avoid some failed paths. In this setting, the trade-off emphasized by entry-timing theory is especially salient.

The result also reinforces Cirik and Makadok's (2023) distinction between observed first-mover advantage and causal first-mover benefit. Firms select into timing positions. Our overlap weighting and fixed effects greatly improve observed comparability, but they do not recreate the counterfactual in which the same firm enters the same topic at a different time. The appropriate conclusion is thus empirical and associational: **a general adjusted early-entry advantage is not visible in this sample**.

### 5.2 Arrival and persistence are different organizational problems

The contrast between H4 and H5 sharpens the paper's theoretical contribution. Network diversity does not predict earlier entry after adjustment. Thus, internationally connected firms are not simply selecting themselves into the early group. Yet the early-entry–persistence relationship is more favorable at higher network diversity across most frozen definitions.

This pattern points toward a distinction between **opportunity sensing** and **post-entry adaptation**. If international diversity primarily helped firms detect scientific opportunities sooner, H4 should be positive. The data do not support that mechanism. The recurring H5 direction instead fits the possibility that heterogeneous external knowledge becomes valuable after the firm has committed to an uncertain field. A diverse network may provide alternative technical perspectives, complementary methods, or channels through which researchers can reorient as a domain changes.

The mechanism remains interpretive. Country-level network entropy is not a direct measure of absorptive capacity, partner quality, or internal integration routines. Nonetheless, the pattern is consistent with the broader argument that external knowledge creates value when firms possess the architecture required to access and recombine it (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990; Fabrizio, 2009; Najafi-Tavani et al., 2018).

### 5.3 A capability-contingent interpretation of scientific timing

A technology-management implication follows. The strategic question is not only whether a firm is early but what it brings to an early position. If scientific first-mover advantage is capability-contingent, firms with identical relative timing can have different prospects because their knowledge access and integration environments differ.

H5 cannot establish this proposition causally, and its inference is sensitive. Yet two aspects justify taking the pattern seriously as theory-building evidence. First, the interaction remains positive under nearly every frozen operational definition. Second, leaving out each of the 21 firms individually never changes the sign. This stability reduces concern that the result is generated by one company or one arbitrary early-entry threshold. At the same time, CRV3 and firm wild-bootstrap inference prevent us from describing the result as robustly significant.

The distinction between sign stability and significance stability is itself useful. A conventional single-specification paper might either emphasize p=.022 and overstate H5 or emphasize a conservative p-value and discard the recurring direction. The frozen design grid reveals both: the relationship is directionally broad and statistically uncertain. For theory development, that is a more informative conclusion than either binary interpretation.

### 5.4 Implications for technology and R&D managers

Managers should be cautious about treating early participation in fashionable scientific domains as a stand-alone innovation strategy. The study does not show that arriving earlier reliably produces more scientific output or greater persistence. Early participation commits researchers and attention under uncertainty, and later entrants may benefit from information generated by pioneers.

The results instead suggest that the managerial decision has at least two dimensions: **when to enter** and **whether the organization possesses sufficient external scientific knowledge access to continue learning after entry**. The latter is not a causal prescription from this study. Rather, it highlights a resource-allocation question managers should consider when evaluating early scientific commitments: an early position without complementary knowledge access may not translate into sustained scientific participation.

### 5.5 Limitations and future research

Several limitations define the paper's scope. First, the corporate adoption frontier is relative to the linked firms; it is not a population-complete record of the first corporate entrant in every topic. Future work should broaden validated firm coverage or construct a more population-complete corporate frontier.

Second, the primary analysis includes only 21 firms. Firm-topic variation supports within-firm and within-topic comparisons, and leave-one-firm-out analysis addresses single-firm dependence, but the sample does not support universal claims about corporate behavior. Replication across a broader corporate population is important.

Third, publication-based entry captures observable scientific production rather than the first moment at which a firm becomes aware of or privately invests in a topic. Firms may conduct unpublished R&D, hire scientists, license knowledge, acquire startups, or collaborate privately before appearing in publication data.

Fourth, entry timing remains endogenous. A stronger causal design would require an exogenous shock, quasi-random access change, or other source of timing variation. The present study instead estimates carefully adjusted associations and incorporates selection and inference uncertainty transparently.

Fifth, H3 cannot be estimated in the primary design because normalized citation impact has inadequate within-topic early/later support. Larger firm-topic publication histories could support more precise tests of scientific influence, including breakthrough citations, diffusion across fields, or downstream patent linkage.

Finally, international network entropy measures geographic diversity but not the quality, technological complementarity, or governance of scientific relationships. Future research should distinguish partner quality, tie strength, university-industry composition, and internal absorptive routines to test the mechanism implied by H5 more directly.

## 6. Conclusion

This study asks whether entering observable corporate scientific production earlier creates a persistent advantage across emerging scientific domains. The answer is not the simple first-mover premium suggested by conventional timing logic. Earlier relative entry is not associated with higher subsequent topic output or greater persistence, and internationally diverse scientific networks do not explain which firms enter earlier. The primary citation-impact contrast is support-limited.

The more interesting pattern is conditional. Across most frozen operational definitions, the early-entry–persistence relationship becomes more favorable as pre-entry international scientific network diversity increases, and the interaction remains positive when every firm is omitted in turn. Yet the statistical evidence is sensitive to small-cluster inference. The result therefore supports a cautious, capability-contingent interpretation rather than a confirmed network effect.

For technology management, the implication is that **being early is not enough**. Early scientific participation may create an opportunity, but the ability to sustain that opportunity appears more plausibly connected to the knowledge architecture surrounding the firm than to timing alone. That boundary condition offers a more realistic foundation for future research on corporate science, strategic timing, and technological innovation.
